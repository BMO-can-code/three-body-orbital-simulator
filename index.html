Choreographies (2000–2013)
Figure-8
Chenciner & Montgomery (2000). Three equal masses chase each other along a figure-8 curve. Found numerically by Moore, proved periodic by Chenciner & Montgomery using variational methods.
Butterfly I–IV
Šuvakov & Dmitrašinović (2013). Four families of symmetric periodic orbits resembling butterfly wings. Discovered by systematic search in initial-condition space.
Moth I–III
Šuvakov & Dmitrašinović (2013). Asymmetric orbits with moth-like trajectories. The two bodies orbit while the third oscillates.
Yarn I–III
Šuvakov & Dmitrašinović (2013). Tight, interwoven orbits resembling wound yarn. Bodies remain close while executing complex loops.
Yin-Yang I–II
Šuvakov & Dmitrašinović (2013). Complementary S-shaped orbits. Each body traces a yin-yang-like path.
Goggles
Šuvakov & Dmitrašinović (2013). Two large loops resembling eyeglasses with a small oscillation between them.
Dragonfly
Šuvakov & Dmitrašinović (2013). Wide wing-like excursions with rapid central oscillations.
Moorish Dance
Šuvakov & Dmitrašinović (2013). Graceful periodic orbit with symmetric body exchanges.
Classical Solutions (1767–1772)
Lagrange Equilateral Triangle
Lagrange (1772). Three equal masses at vertices of an equilateral triangle, all rotating about the center. First known periodic solution. Stable for mass ratios m₁:m₂:m₃ within Routh's criterion.
Euler Collinear
Euler (1767). Three masses on a line, rotating rigidly about the center of mass. The three Euler points L₁, L₂, L₃. Unstable — requires fine-tuning to maintain.
Broucke-Henon Families
Broucke A2 / A4
Broucke & Henon (1970s). Symmetric periodic orbits in the isosceles subclass. Part of a continuum of periodic solutions connected by continuation.
Famous Problems
Burrau (Pythagorean) Problem
Burrau (1913). Masses 3, 4, 5 at vertices of a 3-4-5 right triangle, released from rest. Exhibits violent chaotic scattering with close encounters and eventual ejection. Solved numerically by Szebehely & Peters (1967).
Possible Outcomes of Three-Body Interactions
1. Stable Periodic Orbit
All three bodies return to their initial configuration after one period. Extremely rare — requires fine-tuned initial conditions. Only ~13 families known as of 2013. Energy and angular momentum conserved exactly.
2. Quasi-Periodic Orbit
Bodies never exactly repeat but remain bounded. Orbits fill a torus in phase space. Common near periodic orbits — small perturbations from periodic solutions yield quasi-periodic behavior.
3. Binary + Ejected Single
Most common outcome (~50% of chaotic encounters). Two bodies form a tight binary, the third is ejected to infinity with positive energy. The binary tightens (losing energy to the ejecta) and hardens. This is the "gravitational slingshot."
4. Exchange Interaction
A passing body replaces one member of an existing binary. The original binary partner is ejected. Important in globular cluster dynamics — explains mass segregation and Blue Straggler formation.
5. Resonant Encounters
Bodies undergo multiple close approaches before eventual resolution. Can produce complex trajectories lasting many dynamical times. The system passes through several "democratic" phases where all three interact strongly.
6. Hierarchical Triple
A close binary with a distant third companion. Stable if the outer orbit is ~3× wider than the inner orbit (Mardling-Aarseth criterion). Common in exoplanetary systems and stellar triples (~50% of solar-type stars).
7. Collision / Merger
Two or three bodies collide during a close encounter. In stellar systems: tidal disruption events, stellar mergers, or common envelope evolution. In point-mass simulations: regularized contact. Produces gravitational wave bursts.
8. Ejection of All Three
Rare — all three bodies escape to infinity. Requires initial kinetic energy > |potential energy|. Produces three unbound hyperbolic orbits. Total energy > 0.
9. Figure-8 Type Choreography
All three bodies trace the same curve with time delay. A subset of periodic orbits. The figure-8 is the simplest example. Higher-order choreographies exist (e.g., 3×1, 4×1 symmetry types).
10. Lagrange Configuration (Temporary)
Three bodies form an equilateral triangle that rotates. Unstable in general — small perturbations grow exponentially. However, Trojan asteroids maintain quasi-Lagrange points (L₄, L₅) in the Sun-Jupiter system.
11. Euler Collinear Configuration (Unstable)
Three bodies remain collinear while rotating. Always unstable — any transverse perturbation causes collapse. However, the L₁, L₂, L₃ points are used for spacecraft parking orbits (James Webb at Sun-Earth L₂).
The Three-Body Problem
Problem Statement
Given initial positions & velocities of 3 point masses under Newtonian gravity, predict their motion for all future time.
Why It's Hard
• No general closed-form solution exists (Poincaré 1890 — proved the problem is non-integrable)
• The system has 18 phase-space dimensions (3 bodies × 3 coords × 2 for position+velocity)
• Only 10 integrals of motion exist: 3 energy, 3 momentum, 3 angular momentum, 1 center-of-mass
• Non-integrable = cannot reduce to quadratures. Trajectories are generically chaotic
• Sensitive dependence on initial conditions: δ(t) ~ δ₀ eλt where λ is the Lyapunov exponent
Newton's Law of Gravitation
Fᵢⱼ = G mᵢ mⱼ / |rᵢ - rⱼ|² × r̂ᵢⱼ
Each body feels the gravitational pull from the other two. Forces superpose: F_total = F₁₂ + F₁₃ for body 1. The pull on body i uses the mass of the OTHER body, mⱼ — the most common bug in home-grown N-body code.
Equations of Motion
mᵢ r̈ᵢ = Σⱼ≠ᵢ G mᵢ mⱼ (rⱼ - rᵢ) / |rⱼ - rᵢ|³
Six coupled second-order ODEs (or 12 first-order). No analytic solution for general initial conditions.
Numerical Integration (This Simulation)
• Velocity Verlet (kick–drift–kick): symplectic, 2nd-order and time-reversible
• Adaptive timestep: h = min(hMax, c · r_min / v_rel) — steps shrink automatically during close encounters
• Softening parameter ε keeps the force finite at the closest passes

Conservation Laws
Energy: E = ½ Σ mᵢ vᵢ² - Σ G mᵢ mⱼ / rᵢⱼ = const
Momentum: P = Σ mᵢ vᵢ = const
Angular Momentum: L = Σ mᵢ rᵢ × vᵢ = const
These 10 conserved quantities are the ONLY ones. The system is non-integrable — no additional hidden symmetries exist (Poincaré's theorem).
Chaos & Lyapunov Exponents
• Lyapunov exponent λ measures divergence rate of nearby trajectories
• λ > 0 → chaotic (exponential divergence)
• λ = 0 → regular/quasi-periodic
• Lyapunov time τ = 1/λ → predictability horizon
• For three-body: τ ~ few orbital periods
• "Butterfly effect": rounding errors grow exponentially → long-term prediction impossible
Periodic Orbits & Choreographies
• Periodic orbits satisfy: r(t) = r(t + T) for all bodies
• Choreographies: all bodies trace the same curve with phase shift
• Found by: variational methods, Newton-Raphson root-finding in initial-condition space
• ~13 families known (Šuvakov & Dmitrašinović 2013), initial conditions published to 15 digits
• Many more likely exist — search is computationally expensive
• Symmetry reduction reduces dimensionality (time-reversal, rotational symmetry)
Applications
• Astrophysics: triple star systems, planetary dynamics, galactic nuclei
• Exoplanets: stability of multi-planet systems, Trojan asteroids
• Gravitational waves: three-body mergers in LIGO/Virgo
• Cosmology: dark matter halo dynamics
• Molecular dynamics: three-body scattering in chemical reactions
• Spacecraft: gravity-assist trajectories, parking orbits at Lagrange points
• Mathematics: proof of chaos, topology of phase space, Arnold diffusion
