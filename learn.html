Periodic orbits

Butterfly I and Goggles are not included. At double precision they escape within one period, so presenting them as periodic would be dishonest.
02

What can happen

A three-body encounter resolves into one of a small number of fates. These are the ones worth knowing, roughly ordered from rarest to most destructive.

Stable periodic orbit
All three bodies return to their initial configuration after one period. Extremely rare, and requires fine-tuned initial conditions. Only about 13 families were known as of 2013. Energy and angular momentum are conserved exactly.
Quasi-periodic orbit
Bodies never exactly repeat but remain bounded. Orbits fill a torus in phase space. Common near periodic orbits: a small perturbation from a periodic solution usually yields quasi-periodic motion.
Binary plus an ejected single
The most common outcome of a chaotic encounter (roughly half). Two bodies form a tight binary and the third is ejected with positive energy. The binary tightens as it loses energy to the ejecta. This is the gravitational slingshot.
Exchange interaction
A passing body replaces one member of an existing binary, and the original partner is ejected. Important in globular clusters, where it drives mass segregation and the formation of blue stragglers.
Resonant encounters
Bodies make several close approaches before the system resolves. Trajectories can last many dynamical times, passing through democratic phases in which all three interact strongly.
Hierarchical triple
A close binary with a distant third companion. Stable when the outer orbit is about three times wider than the inner one (the Mardling–Aarseth criterion). Common among stellar triples, which make up roughly half of solar-type systems.
Collision or merger
Two or three bodies collide during a close encounter. In stellar systems this gives tidal disruption, mergers or common-envelope evolution, and in point-mass simulations it is handled by softening. Real collisions would radiate gravitational waves.
Ejection of all three
Rare. All three bodies escape to infinity, which requires the initial kinetic energy to exceed the magnitude of the potential energy. The result is three unbound hyperbolic orbits and positive total energy.
Figure-8 type choreography
All three bodies trace the same curve with a time delay. A subset of periodic orbits, of which the figure-8 is the simplest example. Higher-order choreographies exist, with 3×1 and 4×1 symmetry types.
Lagrange configuration (temporary)
Three bodies form an equilateral triangle that rotates. Unstable in general: small perturbations grow exponentially. Trojan asteroids still maintain quasi-Lagrange motion around L4 and L5 in the Sun–Jupiter system.
Euler collinear configuration (unstable)
Three bodies stay on a line while rotating. Always unstable, since any transverse perturbation causes collapse. The L1, L2 and L3 points are nevertheless useful for spacecraft, including JWST at Sun–Earth L2.

How it works

The simulator runs a symplectic integrator with an adaptive step. These notes cover the equations, the conservation laws, and where the method breaks down.

Problem statement
Given initial positions and velocities of three point masses under Newtonian gravity, predict their motion for all future time.
Why it is hard
• No general closed-form solution exists; Poincaré showed in 1890 that the problem is non-integrable
• The system has 18 phase-space dimensions: 3 bodies × 3 coordinates × 2 for position and velocity
• Only 10 integrals of motion exist: energy, three momentum components, three angular momentum components, and the centre of mass
• Non-integrable means it cannot be reduced to quadratures, and trajectories are generically chaotic
• Nearby trajectories separate as δ(t) ~ δ₀ eλt, where λ is the Lyapunov exponent
Newton's law of gravitation
Fᵢⱼ = G mᵢ mⱼ / |rᵢ − rⱼ|² × r̂ᵢⱼ
Each body feels the pull of the other two, and the forces superpose. The pull on body i uses the mass of the other body, mⱼ. Getting that backwards is the most common bug in home-grown N-body code.
Equations of motion
mᵢ r̈ᵢ = Σⱼ≠ᵢ G mᵢ mⱼ (rⱼ − rᵢ) / |rⱼ − rᵢ|³
Six coupled second-order ODEs, or twelve first-order ones. There is no analytic solution for general initial conditions.
Numerical integration
• Velocity Verlet (kick–drift–kick): symplectic, second order, and time-reversible
• Adaptive timestep h = min(hMax, c · r_min / v_rel), so steps shrink during close encounters
• A softening parameter ε keeps the force finite at the closest passes
• Each preset carries the timestep and softening settings that verify it to t = 400
• Every preset is centre-of-mass corrected at load
• Speed scales the number of adaptive steps per frame, from 0.1× to 30×
Conservation laws
Energy: E = ½ Σ mᵢ vᵢ² − Σ G mᵢ mⱼ / rᵢⱼ = const
Momentum: P = Σ mᵢ vᵢ = const
Angular momentum: L = Σ mᵢ rᵢ × vᵢ = const
These ten quantities are the only ones. The system is non-integrable, so no additional hidden symmetries exist.
Chaos and Lyapunov exponents
• The Lyapunov exponent λ measures how fast nearby trajectories separate
• λ > 0 means chaotic, with exponential divergence; λ = 0 means regular or quasi-periodic
• The Lyapunov time τ = 1/λ sets the predictability horizon
• For the three-body problem τ is a few orbital periods
• Rounding errors grow exponentially, so long-term prediction is impossible in principle
Where this method struggles
• Softening changes the physics of very close encounters; it is a stabiliser, not a solution
• Double precision sets a hard predictability horizon even for verified orbits
• Lagrange and Euler are exact but linearly unstable, so their eventual breakup is physical, not a bug
• Butterfly I and Goggles escape within one period at this precision, which is why they are omitted
